openMemristorPDK · beginner walkthrough

Your First Memristor Simulation

From a closed laptop to a real simulated memristor curve on your screen (and a look at the chip itself), in 24 steps (plus a one-time install on a new computer). Every step has a real screenshot from this exact setup. You only ever need to click whatever is inside the red box, or type exactly what is in a black box.

Part 0 · only once, on a new computer · 30–60 minutes, mostly waiting

Install everything from scratch

Skip this part if openPDKcreator already works on this computer. On a new Linux computer (Ubuntu or similar), these five steps download everything: two programs, the openPDKcreator tool, the memristor PDK, and the "container" with all the chip-design programs. You need an internet connection and about 35 GB of free disk space. Do them in a terminal: press Ctrl + Alt + T to open one.

A

Install git and podman

Type these two lines, pressing Enter after each. The computer asks for your password (the one you log in with). Nothing shows on screen while you type it, that's normal.

$ sudo apt update
$ sudo apt install -y git podman
What these aregit downloads projects from GitHub. podman runs the container, the separate computer-inside-the-computer where all the chip-design programs live.
B

Download openPDKcreator

This makes a folder and downloads the openPDKcreator tool into it, as openPDKcreator. It's the same folder step 2 uses later.

$ mkdir -p ~/Documentos/rodrigo
$ cd ~/Documentos/rodrigo
$ git clone https://github.com/rpicos-uib/openpdkcreator-toolkit.git openPDKcreator
You should seeOne line, in your computer's language. On a Spanish computer:
Clonando en 'openPDKcreator'...
C

Download the memristor PDK

The memristor PDK goes inside the openPDKcreator folder, as openMemristorPDK:

$ cd openPDKcreator
$ git clone https://github.com/rpicos-uib/openMemristorPDK.git
Clonando en 'openMemristorPDK'...
D

Download the container launcher

IIC-OSIC-TOOLS, from the university in Linz that builds the chip-design container, has the script that starts it. openPDKcreator looks for it in ~/eda/IIC-OSIC-TOOLS:

$ git clone --depth 1 https://github.com/iic-jku/IIC-OSIC-TOOLS.git ~/eda/IIC-OSIC-TOOLS
E

Start it for the first time

Still in the openPDKcreator folder, run the same start command as step 2:

$ ./start_eda_container.sh --gui-memristor

Part 1 · about 2 minutes

Switch everything on

All the chip-design programs live inside a "container", which is like a separate computer running inside this one. You start it with one command and then look at its screen through your web browser.

Open Firefox (or Chrome) and go to this address. Type it exactly, with 127.0.0.1 and not localhost:

If it says "unable to connect"Check you typed 127.0.0.1 and 8081, then go back to step 2 and make sure the command finished without an error.
4

Make the program window big

After a few seconds the openPDKcreator window appears. It's too small to show all its buttons, so click the square button at its top-right corner to make it fill the screen.

TipDouble-clicking the window's title bar does the same thing.
5

The program is ready

Nothing to click here, just a look around. The program opens on the Wizard tab: a map of everything the PDK contains. Green boxes are parts that are already finished. You don't need this map today.

While it starts, openPDKcreator also compiles the memristor recipes (step 10 shows one) into files the simulator can load. That happens by itself, there's nothing to click. The first time, the bar at the very bottom of the window says Compilation performed correctly.

What's a PDK?A Process Design Kit is the instruction manual a chip factory gives to chip designers: which materials exist, how small things can be, and how each device behaves electrically. This one describes a made-up factory that builds memristors, resistors whose resistance depends on how much current has gone through them before. They "remember".
Part 2 · about 2 minutes

Find the memristor

The Library Manager is like a parts catalogue. We'll open the gold/titanium-dioxide/gold memristor and read the little program that describes how it behaves.

6

Open the Library Manager

Click the Library Manager tab, near the top-left.

7

Pick the library

In the Libraries list on the left, click openmemristorpdk_pr (real). It's the only one there.

8

Pick the memristor

The middle list (Cells) fills up with parts. Click memristor_tio2_au (9 view(s)).

What the name meanstio2 is titanium dioxide, the material in the middle that actually switches. au is gold (from the Latin aurum), used for the electrodes on top and bottom. So this part is a gold / TiO₂ / gold sandwich.
9

Open the memristor's behaviour file

The right side now lists every "view" of this part: its drawing, its layout, its models... Find the Verilog-A row and click its View button.

10

Read the behaviour file, then close it

A window shows the file. This is the "recipe" the simulator uses to work out how much current flows through the memristor. When you've had a look, click Close. Nothing else happens: the recipe was already compiled when the program started (step 5).

openMemristorPDK/user_models/veriloga/memristor_tio2_au.vaVerilog-A (the comment lines at the top are left out)
`include "disciplines.vams"

module memristor_tio2_au(BE, TE);
inout BE, TE;
electrical BE, TE;

parameter real Ron = 100 from (0:inf);
parameter real Roff = 16e3 from (0:inf);
parameter real D = 10n from (0:inf);
parameter real uv = 1e-14 from (0:inf);
parameter real x0 = 0.1 from [0:1];

real x;
real Rmem;

analog begin
  x = min(max(idt((uv * Ron / (D * D)) * I(BE, TE), x0), 0.0), 1.0);
  Rmem = Ron * x + Roff * (1.0 - x);
  I(BE, TE) <+ V(BE, TE) / Rmem;
end
endmodule
BE, TE
The two wires of the memristor: Bottom Electrode and Top Electrode.
Ron, Roff
Its lowest and highest resistance: 100 Ω when fully "on", 16 000 Ω when fully "off".
D
How thick the TiO₂ layer is: 10 nanometres, about 1/10 000 of a hair.
x
The memory. A number between 0 and 1 saying how much of the layer has been switched on. idt(...) means "add up over time", so x grows while current flows one way and shrinks when it flows back.
Rmem
Today's resistance, a mix between Roff and Ron depending on x.
I <+ V / Rmem
Ohm's law: current = voltage ÷ resistance. The only twist is that the resistance keeps changing.
Only if you change the recipeIf you click Edit, change something and Save, closing the window compiles it again and tells you how it went: Compilation performed correctly, or the line with the mistake. Today you don't need to change anything.
Part 3 · about 1 minute

Open the test circuit

To see what a memristor does, we connect it to a battery that keeps flipping between +1 V and −1 V (a sine wave), and measure the current.

11

Pick the test circuit

In the Cells list, click memristor_tio2_au_tb (1 view(s)). The tb stands for testbench, a circuit made for testing a part.

12

Open it in xschem

On the Schematic row, click Open in xschem. xschem is the program for drawing circuits.

13

Meet the test circuit

A black xschem window opens on top. Wait until you can see the drawing: the very first time it can take up to half a minute, so don't click anything else meanwhile. The small box on the left is the memristor n1. The circle on the right is the sine-wave source V1. They're joined by a wire called te. The text below the circuit tells the simulator what to do.

16

The result: a memristor's fingerprint

Two new windows appear. The small white one in the middle is the simulator (ngspice) reporting what it did. The black one on the right is the result.

How to read it Left/right is the voltage (−1 V to +1 V). Up/down is the current, in millionths of an amp (µA). A normal resistor would draw one straight line: the same current every time you're at the same voltage. The memristor draws two different paths, one going up and one coming back. At the same voltage it lets more or less current through depending on its history. That's its memory. Both paths cross exactly at zero. Scientists call this a pinched hysteresis loop, and it's the official fingerprint of a memristor.

Part 6 · about 2 minutes

See what the chip looks like

The simulation showed how the memristor behaves. The layout shows how it is built: the shapes the factory would actually print onto the chip, seen from above.

20

Pick the memristor again

In the Cells list, click memristor_tio2_au (9 view(s)), the same part as in step 8.

21

Open the layout in KLayout

On the GDS/Layout row, click Open in KLayout. KLayout is the program for looking at chip layouts. It takes a few seconds to appear, at the top-left of the screen.

22

Look at the chip from above

Nothing to click. The big hatched square is the memristor seen from above. It's 1 µm on each side, a thousandth of a millimetre, so about 80 of them would fit across one human hair. The little ruler at the bottom-left shows 0.2 µm.

How to read it The memristor is a sandwich of three squares stacked exactly on top of each other, so from above they look like one square. The Layers list (red box) names them. The bold ones are used by this part: BE_Au is the gold bottom electrode, MEM_TiO2 the 10 nm titanium-dioxide layer in the middle, and TE_Au the gold top electrode. The grey names are layers the other memristors in this PDK use: hafnium dioxide, titanium and aluminium. Each layer has its own colour and hatch pattern, which is why you can see the lines of all three layers crossing.

The layer's name shown in the Layers list.
